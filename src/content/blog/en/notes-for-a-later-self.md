The notebook costs less than a coffee. The pen costs more than I would like to admit, and it is the only piece of stationery I have ever felt protective of. Together they do something none of my apps have managed: they slow me down enough to think.

## Notes are for the person I will be later

Almost nothing I write is for the present. It is for the version of me who will open this page in two years and wonder what I was so worked up about.

That changes what is worth writing down. A task list expires by Friday. A sentence like _"the reason this felt urgent was that I was afraid of being wrong in public"_ does not. It keeps its value because it records a reason, not an instruction.

So I try to write the second kind. Not what I decided, but what made the decision feel necessary at the time.

## What actually survives

Looking back over a few notebooks, three things keep showing up:

- **A sentence I read somewhere** and copied out by hand, because copying forces me to notice the shape of it.
- **A question I could not answer**, written down so it stops circling.
- **A small observation** — the way light fell on a wall, the exact sound of a gate — that has no use at all and is the reason I keep the notebook.

Everything else, the plans and schedules, turns out to have been written somewhere else too.

## The paper is the point

Screens reward speed. Paper does not. A page has a fixed size, so writing on it is a series of small decisions about what deserves the space — and that editing, done in the moment, is most of the thinking.

The pen runs out eventually. I have started buying the same one twice, which is either a sensible habit or a small superstition. Probably both.
